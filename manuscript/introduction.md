There is a need for methods to search for loci where a methylation lesion on a single homolog causes disease — on its own, or in trans with a genetic mutation on the other homolog. Yet standard methods report an unphased methylation level at each CpG, which integrates over both homologs and so cannot, on its own, identify a change confined to one of them. Accordingly, a *heterozygously methylated* site — methylated on one homolog, unmethylated on the other — acquires an intermediate methylation level (Fig 1A), indistinguishable from a homozygously partially-methylated site, such as arises from somatic mosaicism or incomplete maintenance methylation. Partitioning reads by haplotype — enabled by PacBio HiFi over long read-backed phase blocks — splits the pooled methylation track in two and resolves the heterozygous state (Fig 1B). If, in addition, we can trace each of these two haplotypes back to the parental homolog it descends from, we can ask whether a homolog-specific methylation lesion segregates with disease in a pedigree at any CpG in the genome.

That such homolog-specific methylation lesions cause disease is already established at a handful of known loci, across two distinct classes. The first class is a homolog-specific loss of methylation (LOM) at an imprinted locus ([Monk et al. 2019, Nat Rev Genet](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41576-018-0092-0)). At most imprinted loci the methylated allele is maternal, so the disease-causing event is a maternal LOM (Fig 1C): maternal LOM at the *KCNQ1OT1* locus accounts for roughly half of Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome cases ([Brioude et al. 2018, Nat Rev Endocrinol](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrendo.2017.166)); maternal LOM at the *PLAGL1* locus underlies transient neonatal diabetes mellitus ([Temple & Mackay, GeneReviews](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK1534/)); maternal LOM at the *GNAS* locus underlies pseudohypoparathyroidism ([Mantovani et al. 2018, Nat Rev Endocrinol](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41574-018-0042-0)); and LOM across several maternally methylated loci is the predominant signature of multi-locus imprinting disturbance ([Mackay et al. 2008, Nat Genet](https://www.nature.com/articles/ng.187)). The mirror case occurs at paternally methylated loci: paternal LOM at the *H19/IGF2* locus underlies Silver-Russell syndrome ([Wakeling et al. 2017, Nat Rev Endocrinol](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrendo.2016.138)), and paternal LOM at the *MEG3/DLK1* locus underlies Temple syndrome ([Gazdagh et al. 2026, GeneReviews](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK621564/)).
The second locus class is the compound genetic–epigenetic heterozygote (Fig 1D), in which a gene is inactivated by a sequence variant on one haplotype and a methylation lesion on the other. For example, in the disorder epi-cblC, a coding loss-of-function in *MMACHC* sits in trans with hypermethylation silencing the other *MMACHC* allele ([Guéant et al. 2018, Nat Commun](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-017-02306-5)).

Diagnostic infrastructure for these known disorders is locus-targeted: neither the CpGs it interrogates nor the parent-of-origin resolution it provides extends genome-wide. The imprinting disorders are diagnosed by methylation-sensitive MLPA at the known imprinting control regions ([Mackay et al. 2022, Clin Epigenetics](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13148-022-01358-9)); epi-cblC by *MMACHC* sequencing plus *PRDX1*/*MMACHC* methylation testing ([Guéant et al. 2018, Nat Commun](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-017-02306-5)). Each workup asks whether a *given* locus carries the expected lesion in a given patient. Broader approaches are beginning to be deployed, most prominently Illumina Infinium methylation arrays that recognise rare syndromes by their distinctive multi-CpG methylation signatures ([Sadikovic et al. 2021, Genet Med](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1098360021052102)). But these arrays sample roughly 850,000 pre-selected CpG positions — a small fraction of the ~28 million CpGs in the human genome — and, like the locus-specific workups they supplement, do not resolve methylation to a specific parental homolog across an entire pedigree. So while the catalog of *known* disease-causing homolog-specific methylation lesions is small and existing testing serves it well, the catalog of *unknown* loci — where the same two classes of lesion may cause disease but no targeted assay yet exists — is invisible to current workflows. Closing this discovery gap requires the methylation state at every CpG on *each specific physical homolog* of every pedigree member, with each homolog traceable across generations to a parent of origin.

Tapestry is a workflow that produces exactly this output. Genetic phasing has long resolved variants onto parental haplotypes ([Abecasis et al. 2002, Nat Genet](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng786); [Roach et al. 2011, Am J Hum Genet](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2011.07.023)); Tapestry extends that inheritance logic to methylation. Built on PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing — which reports a methylation call at every CpG it covers, rather than at a pre-selected array subset — Tapestry phases methylation onto the founder haplotypes of a sequenced human pedigree (including the trio special case), so that for every member, the methylation level at every CpG in the genome is resolved onto a specific parental homolog. With this output in hand, both classes of lesion become discoverable genome-wide through short downstream queries, scanning for parent-of-origin-restricted methylation changes that segregate with phenotype (Fig. 1C), and for cases where a coding loss-of-function and a methylation lesion sit in trans on opposite homologs (Fig. 1D).
